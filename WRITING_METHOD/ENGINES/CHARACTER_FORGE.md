# The Character Forge
**Module:** Living Story Method (LSM)  
**Purpose:** Create characters who feel like specific people with independent motives, credible behavior, distinct voices, meaningful relationships, and the capacity to change the story.

This is a working method, not a form to fill mechanically. Use the depth appropriate to the character's importance. Major characters need a complete dossier; recurring supporting characters need enough detail to act independently; one-scene characters need only the details that make their presence convincing.

## 1. Prime directive

Do not create a character as a bundle of traits, a job title, a power set, a trope, or a tool for the protagonist. Create a person whose desires, beliefs, history, limitations, habits, relationships, and choices generate behavior.

A useful character model is:

**Person = desire + worldview + history + contradiction + strategy + limits + relationships + capacity for change.**

Every major character must be capable of making a choice the author did not need merely to move the plot, provided that choice follows from who the character is.

## 2. The character dossier

For every major or recurring character, develop the following sections. Do not leave core sections vague just because a character is mysterious. Mystery should be deliberately controlled, not a substitute for design.

### A. Identity and story role
- Name, aliases, pronunciation if relevant, age or age range, pronouns where relevant, occupation, status, affiliations, origin, and current circumstances.
- Narrative role: protagonist, rival, ally, antagonist, mentor, foil, witness, catalyst, or a more specific combination.
- What the character changes in the story, and what the story pressures them to change.
- Public reputation versus the reality known to the reader or revealed over time.
- Canon/source constraints for fanfiction, including what is established, inferred, deliberately altered, or still unknown.

### B. Core psychology
- **Want:** the concrete thing they consciously pursue.
- **Need:** the deeper adjustment, truth, or capacity that would help them, whether or not they recognize it.
- **Fear:** what outcome they most want to avoid.
- **Wound or formative pressure:** an experience, pattern, loss, or environment that shaped them. Avoid forcing every character to have a melodramatic trauma.
- **Belief about the world:** the rule they think keeps them safe or successful.
- **Self-image:** who they believe they are.
- **Private truth:** what their actions reveal that their self-image conceals.
- **Values and hierarchy:** what they protect first when values conflict.
- **Contradiction:** two qualities, desires, or beliefs that coexist and cause friction.
- **Emotional blind spot:** what they consistently misread about themselves or others.
- **Shame, pride, longing, envy, guilt, or resentment:** select only what genuinely fits.
- **Thresholds:** what they will not do, what they claim they will not do, and what pressure might make them cross a line.

### C. Agency and strategy
- Immediate, short-term, and long-term goals.
- Their default strategy when they want something: charm, force, patience, analysis, deception, bargaining, avoidance, service, humor, intimidation, or another individualized approach.
- Secondary tactics when the first strategy fails.
- What they do when they lose control.
- What they do when they have power over someone.
- What they do when they are wrong, embarrassed, ignored, threatened, or offered an unexpected advantage.
- What they are willing to sacrifice, and what they miscalculate the cost of sacrificing.
- Independent plans that continue off-page and can affect future events.

### D. Competence, resources, and limits
- Skills supported by evidence, training, experience, or setting-specific logic.
- Knowledge they possess and how they acquired it.
- Resources, allies, money, authority, equipment, influence, and access.
- Physical, psychological, social, ethical, informational, and practical limitations.
- Blind spots and predictable mistakes.
- Costs, risks, counters, and failure conditions for any powers or special abilities.
- What they cannot do, cannot know, cannot obtain quickly, or cannot safely attempt.
- Separate **claimed competence**, **demonstrated competence**, and **actual limits**. Do not make every protagonist secretly excellent at everything.

### E. History and formative influences
- A concise life chronology, including only events that affect present behavior or future plot.
- Family, upbringing, education, culture, class, community, mentors, rivals, and formative institutions.
- Important successes, failures, betrayals, obligations, and turning points.
- What the character remembers accurately, misremembers, hides, or interprets differently.
- Which past events are canon facts versus working assumptions.
- How history affects habits and decisions now. Backstory should explain behavior, not excuse every action or appear as an information dump.

### F. Relationships
For every significant relationship, record:
- What this character wants from the other person.
- What they believe the other person wants from them.
- Trust, fear, affection, resentment, dependence, competition, attraction, obligation, or leverage.
- What can be said openly and what remains unspoken.
- The power balance and how it changes across contexts.
- A shared history, private reference, unresolved wound, or recurring point of friction.
- What each person misunderstands about the other.
- What event could strengthen, weaken, or permanently change the relationship.

Do not make relationships emotionally symmetrical by default. Two people may care in different ways, want incompatible futures, or interpret the same moment differently.

### G. Behavior and embodied personality
Define observable behavior instead of relying on labels such as “cold,” “kind,” “smart,” or “confident.”
- Speech habits, pace, vocabulary, directness, humor, evasions, and silence.
- Posture, movement, gestures, eye contact, personal space, and use of objects.
- What they notice first in a room and what they overlook.
- How they behave with superiors, equals, dependents, strangers, and people they fear.
- Stress responses: fight, flee, freeze, appease, joke, analyze, dissociate, over-control, or an individual mixture.
- Habits that emerge under pressure, not random quirks added for decoration.
- Small preferences and routines that make daily life specific.
- The gap between emotion felt, emotion shown, and emotion correctly interpreted by observers.

### H. Voice fingerprint
Give every important speaking character a distinct voice profile:
- Sentence length and rhythm.
- Vocabulary, idioms, technical language, slang, and formality.
- Directness versus implication.
- Humor style and what they find funny.
- Preferred conversational tactics: question, command, tease, lecture, bargain, deflect, confess, flatter, or threaten.
- How their speech changes with status, intimacy, exhaustion, anger, fear, or deception.
- Words or patterns they overuse, if natural.
- Things they would not plausibly say.
- A short sample of dialogue that demonstrates the voice without turning it into a catchphrase machine.

Voice must be recognizable through choices and rhythm, not merely repeated verbal tics. Never give every character the same polished, witty, perfectly articulate dialogue.

### I. Character arc and change
- Starting state: the current strategy, belief, and relationship pattern.
- Pressure: events that challenge the strategy or expose its cost.
- Decision points: choices that reveal character and make change possible.
- Possible direction: growth, corruption, hardening, recovery, disillusionment, self-acceptance, tragic repetition, or deliberate refusal to change.
- Evidence of change: altered behavior, priorities, tactics, relationships, or self-understanding.
- Resistance: why change is difficult and what the character loses by changing.
- End-state possibilities without forcing a predetermined moral lesson.

A character may change gradually, regress, learn the wrong lesson, or remain stable while the world changes around them. Change must be caused by experience and choices, not merely announced.

### J. Narrative function and story pressure
- What unique conflict, knowledge, capability, worldview, or emotional pressure they bring.
- What scenes or choices become impossible if they are removed.
- How they challenge or reveal the protagonist without existing only for that purpose.
- What they do when the protagonist is absent.
- What independent objective could make them oppose the protagonist while still being reasonable.
- Their secrets, reveal conditions, and consequences, with the difference between a secret's truth and characters' beliefs clearly tracked.

### K. Continuity and progression record
Track stable facts, changing facts, unresolved facts, and source confidence:
- Stable identity and appearance anchors.
- Current age, location, injuries, resources, relationships, knowledge, and status.
- Promises, debts, secrets, plans, and decisions.
- Skills and abilities actually demonstrated versus merely suspected.
- Current emotional state and what caused it.
- Canon fact / project canon / established inference / working plan / speculation.
- Last confirmed state and the event that changed it.

## 3. The pressure-test suite

Before treating a major character as ready, answer these questions with concrete behavior:

1. What do they do when humiliated in public?
2. What do they do when frightened but watched?
3. How do they react when someone outsmarts them?
4. What happens when they are betrayed by someone they need?
5. How do they behave when they have power and no immediate consequences?
6. What opportunity would tempt them to violate their own principles?
7. What kindness would they mistrust, and what cruelty would they rationalize?
8. What do they misunderstand about the person they care about most?
9. What do they do when their first plan fails?
10. What would make them walk away from a goal they have pursued for years?
11. What do they do when no one is watching?
12. What evidence would change their mind, and what evidence would they reject?

These are not arbitrary personality tests. Use the answers to generate choices, conflict, and believable inconsistency.

## 4. Character creation workflow

1. **Find the story pressure.** Identify the conflict, world, genre promise, and narrative role the character must serve.
2. **Create a human premise.** Combine a specific desire, a worldview, a contradiction, and a strategy. Avoid building from trope alone.
3. **Establish agency.** Give the character goals and plans that exist independently of the protagonist.
4. **Set competence and limits.** Define what they can actually do, why they can do it, and what it costs.
5. **Build history selectively.** Choose formative experiences that explain present patterns and plant useful future pressure.
6. **Design relationships.** Make each important relationship specific, unequal in some way, and capable of changing.
7. **Build voice and behavior.** Define observable, repeatable patterns that emerge naturally under different pressures.
8. **Create the visual identity.** Use the Living Appearance Method as a companion module, not a substitute for psychology.
9. **Pressure-test.** Simulate decisions under fear, failure, temptation, intimacy, power, and uncertainty.
10. **Plan change and continuity.** Record starting state, potential change, stable facts, and open questions.
11. **Test originality.** Remove superficial quirks and ask whether the character's choices still feel specific.
12. **Use in scenes.** Reveal the dossier through behavior, dialogue, choices, sensory attention, relationships, and consequences. Do not dump the dossier into the prose.

## 5. Scene-use rules

Before writing a scene with a major character, know:
- Their immediate objective.
- What they know, suspect, misunderstand, and are hiding.
- What they want from each person present.
- Their emotional pressure and physical condition.
- Their likely opening tactic and what could make them change tactics.
- The outcome they fear and the cost they are willing to pay.
- What they will remember from the scene afterward.

During revision, ask whether the character made choices or merely delivered information. A character's personality should shape the scene's tactics, timing, attention, mistakes, and consequences.

## 6. Anti-patterns to reject

- Trait lists with no behavioral evidence.
- Tragic backstory used as a shortcut to depth.
- Characters who exist only to praise, assist, teach, or validate the protagonist.
- Sudden competence unsupported by training, resources, or established rules.
- Convenient stupidity in rivals and antagonists.
- Identical voices with different names.
- Contradictions that are random rather than psychologically or situationally motivated.
- An “arc” that consists only of becoming stronger.
- A dossier that is never used to shape decisions.
- Excessive secrets that have no consequences.
- Invented facts silently treated as canon.
- Characters whose every action is optimized for the plot rather than shaped by their own priorities.

## 7. Acceptance standard

A major character is ready when the writer can explain what they want, why they want it, how they pursue it, what they misunderstand, what limits them, how they behave under pressure, what they want from other people, what they would choose without the protagonist present, and how the story can change them. Their appearance, voice, skills, relationships, and continuity must be specific enough that the same person can be recognized across scenes without repeating the same description or behavior.

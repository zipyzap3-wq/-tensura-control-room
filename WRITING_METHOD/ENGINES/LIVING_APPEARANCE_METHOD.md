# The Living Appearance Method
**Module:** Living Story Method (LSM)  
**Purpose:** Design specific, memorable, physically coherent character appearances and translate those designs into natural prose, consistent visual references, and continuity-safe descriptions.

This module exists because “tall, handsome, black hair, sharp eyes” is not a complete visual design. A character should have an identifiable physical structure, a deliberate silhouette, specific features, an embodied way of moving, clothing with context, and stable details that remain consistent across chapters and visual references.

The method produces three distinct deliverables for every major character:
1. **Full Appearance Dossier:** the detailed reference record for the writer.
2. **Reader-Facing Description:** selective prose filtered through the viewpoint character and scene.
3. **Visual Reference Specification:** a concise, stable brief for artists, image generation, portraits, turnaround sheets, and outfit variations.

Do not confuse these deliverables. The dossier is comprehensive; prose is selective; the visual specification prioritizes repeatable visual anchors.

## 1. Prime directive: design a person, not a checklist

Appearance should feel like a coherent human (or nonhuman) body, not disconnected adjectives. Features should work together: age, ancestry where relevant to the setting, lifestyle, health, work, climate, grooming, resources, culture, personality, and self-presentation may influence what is visible. Do not assume appearance dictates morality, intelligence, personality, or worth.

Choose details intentionally. Not every character needs a scar, unusual eye color, dramatic hairstyle, or “signature” accessory. Distinctiveness comes from the combination of features and how the character inhabits them.

## 2. Full Appearance Dossier

For every major or recurring character, record the following layers. If a detail is unknown or intentionally hidden, mark it as unknown or concealed instead of silently inventing it. If a source establishes a fact, preserve it exactly.

### A. Physical foundation
- Exact age or age range at the current story point; distinguish actual age from apparent age where relevant.
- Height, build, proportions, and overall body composition.
- Shoulder width, torso length, limb proportions, hand size, neck, and other silhouette-relevant details.
- Body type described precisely without relying on vague labels alone.
- Physical development, disability, chronic limitations, prosthetics, scars, or nonhuman anatomy when relevant and established.
- How their body compares with the people and environment around them.
- Changes caused by age, training, injury, illness, transformation, or supernatural effects.

Do not invent exact measurements merely to create false precision. Use measurements when the project needs them; otherwise use stable comparative descriptions.

### B. Skin and complexion
- Skin tone using clear, respectful language appropriate to the setting.
- Undertone where visually relevant.
- Texture, freckles, moles, birthmarks, blemishes, calluses, scars, sun exposure, or other distinguishing details.
- Where details appear and whether they are permanent, temporary, concealed, or source-established.
- How lighting, weather, exertion, embarrassment, injury, or illness may temporarily change the visible surface.

Avoid reducing a person to a skin-tone label. Do not infer ethnicity, ancestry, or identity solely from complexion.

### C. Facial architecture
Describe the structure, not merely the mood:
- Overall face shape and proportions.
- Forehead, brow, cheekbones, cheeks, jaw, chin, and facial symmetry or asymmetry.
- Eyes: shape, spacing, size, color if established, eyelids, lashes, gaze habits, and any unusual feature.
- Brows: shape, density, and habitual movement.
- Nose: bridge, width, length, tip, and distinctive contours.
- Mouth: shape, lips, resting expression, smile, and tension patterns.
- Teeth, dimples, facial hair, piercings, facial marks, or makeup if relevant.
- One or two highly recognizable features that make the face identifiable in a crowd.

“Sharp eyes” is not sufficient by itself. Clarify the actual visible shape or feature, then show what the gaze does in a scene. Avoid making every character's eyes “piercing,” “smoldering,” or “intense.”

### D. Hair
- Natural or established color, including roots or variation if relevant.
- Texture, density, curl pattern, strand behavior, and volume.
- Length and exact shape or cut.
- Hairline, parting, fringe, layers, tied sections, braids, locs, twists, shaved areas, or other construction.
- Grooming, styling products, ornaments, wraps, ties, clips, or protective styles where relevant.
- How the hair moves, reacts to weather, or changes during action.
- Whether the style is practical, cultural, expressive, status-related, or simply personal.
- Continuity rules for hair changes over time.

Describe textured hair specifically and accurately. Do not treat all curls, coils, locs, braids, or protective styles as interchangeable.

### E. Body details and physical condition
- Hands, fingers, nails, wrists, posture, gait, and habitual tension.
- Calluses, ink, scars, tattoos, burns, old injuries, or work-related marks.
- Voice is documented in the Character Forge, but note visible physical cues that affect how speech is produced where relevant.
- Signs of fatigue, training, age, stress, or illness when actually supported by the story.
- Mobility, balance, coordination, and any established physical limitations.
- Temporary states: wet clothing, dust, sweat, blood, bruising, dirt, trembling, or dishevelment. Track when these appear and when they are cleaned or healed.

Never treat temporary damage or dirt as a permanent design detail.

### F. Silhouette and first-read impression
- What is recognizable at a distance: outline, proportions, hairstyle, clothing shape, posture, carried object, or movement.
- The visual impression created by the whole design, expressed without reducing the character to a stereotype.
- What separates them from other characters of similar age, build, status, or genre role.
- How the silhouette changes with coat, armor, uniform, weapons, equipment, or alternate forms.
- What remains recognizable when the face is hidden or the character is seen in shadow.

A strong silhouette does not require an exaggerated body. It requires a readable combination of shape, posture, and visual rhythm.

### G. Movement signature and physical presence
- Resting posture and how they stand, sit, walk, run, turn, and occupy space.
- Pace, stride, balance, weight distribution, and degree of stillness or fidgeting.
- Gestures and habitual use of hands, face, clothing, or objects.
- How they approach people, protect space, retreat, or react to sudden movement.
- Signs of training or profession only when justified by experience and setting.
- Differences between relaxed, performing, frightened, angry, exhausted, injured, or trying to appear confident.
- What other people might notice about their presence and what a particular viewpoint character would notice first.

Movement is often more memorable than another adjective about the face. Do not turn a character's signature movement into a repeated mannerism in every scene.

### H. Wardrobe identity
Document outfits separately rather than pretending a character has one permanent costume:
- Typical outfit and context: daily wear, work, school, combat, ceremony, travel, sleep, disguise, or formal event.
- Garments, layers, fit, cut, length, shape, and how they sit on the body.
- Colors and patterns, materials, weight, texture, shine, wear, repairs, and construction.
- Footwear, gloves, jewelry, bags, belts, tools, weapons, insignia, and accessories.
- Fit with climate, activity, culture, status, income, profession, and personal taste.
- Cleanliness, maintenance, damage, modifications, and what the character refuses to discard.
- What is visible, concealed, secured, or likely to move during action.
- Changes between outfits and the narrative reason for each change.

Clothing should be usable in the scene. Consider heat, rain, movement, armor gaps, pockets, footwear, noise, weight, and whether the character can plausibly perform the action described.

### I. Visual signature and recognition anchors
Select a small set of reliable anchors, usually three to six:
- A distinctive but plausible facial feature.
- A specific hair shape or arrangement.
- A recognizable silhouette or posture.
- A stable accessory, garment, color grouping, or piece of equipment.
- A characteristic movement.
- A distinctive mark or nonhuman feature, if established.

The anchors should reinforce one another rather than compete. Not every anchor must be mentioned in every scene. Use them when the viewpoint naturally notices them or when the reader needs orientation.

### J. Context and change states
Define how the appearance changes under different conditions:
- Lighting and viewing distance.
- Weather, temperature, environment, and physical exertion.
- Relaxed versus tense posture.
- Clean, disheveled, injured, disguised, dressed for combat, or dressed for ceremony.
- Aging, haircut, wardrobe changes, scars, transformations, or lasting injuries.
- What changes quickly and what requires time.
- What remains recognizable through every state.

A character's appearance can evolve, but each change needs a cause and must persist until another event changes it.

### K. Continuity locks
List exact facts that must not drift:
- Stable age, height range, body proportions, skin tone, eye color if known, hair color/texture/style, face shape, scars, tattoos, and handedness if relevant.
- Which side of the face or body carries each distinguishing mark.
- Outfit pieces and accessories for a specific scene.
- What is hidden by clothing, hair, armor, a mask, or an eyepatch.
- Injury state, healing progress, dirt, wetness, blood, and damaged clothing.
- Which features are canon, which are project-established, which are inference, and which remain undecided.
- Deliberate transformations and the exact point in the timeline at which they occur.

For fanfiction, source canon overrides a convenient visual invention unless the project explicitly establishes an intentional divergence. Never silently change an established eye color, scar location, hairstyle, age, or outfit.

## 3. The reader-facing description method

A complete dossier is not a paragraph to paste into a chapter. Translate it into selective, scene-specific prose.

### Step 1: Choose the viewpoint filter
Ask who is looking, what they know, how close they are, and what they care about. A rival may notice posture and threat; a friend may notice fatigue or a familiar expression; a stranger may notice status markers; a viewpoint character in a hurry may register only silhouette and movement.

The description must not include details the viewpoint could not see or reasonably know, unless the narration is intentionally omniscient.

### Step 2: Establish the first visual read
When a character enters or first matters, choose one to three details that quickly orient the reader:
- silhouette or height relative to the scene,
- movement or posture,
- one distinctive face, hair, clothing, or accessory feature.

Do not unload the entire dossier at first sight. Give the reader a clear mental image and add details when they become relevant.

### Step 3: Layer details across action
Reveal further features through:
- movement, conversation, proximity, gestures, grooming, changing light, clothing interaction, or another character's reaction;
- details that affect action, such as wet sleeves, restrictive armor, a hidden hand, a limp, or a loose strap;
- moments of intimacy or conflict that make a small feature newly meaningful.

Do not make every physical detail symbolic. Sometimes a feature is simply part of a person.

### Step 4: Use concrete language
Prefer precise nouns and verbs over stacks of vague adjectives. Replace “he looked intimidating” with observable details that make the viewpoint interpret him as intimidating. Replace generic beauty labels with a specific combination of structure, expression, movement, grooming, and context.

Avoid repetitive beauty metaphors, lists of body parts, and identical descriptions for different characters. Do not describe every person as having sharp eyes, perfect features, a sculpted jaw, or an unreadable expression.

### Step 5: Let attention reveal the observer
Description can reveal the viewpoint character's attraction, envy, suspicion, professional eye, insecurity, cultural expectations, or distraction. This does not mean every description must be metaphorical. The observer's priorities should guide selection.

### Step 6: Maintain spatial clarity
During action, establish where the body is, how it moves, what is visible, and what obstructs it. Do not interrupt a fast sequence with an unrelated full-body portrait. Use appearance details when they clarify distance, leverage, disguise, injury, recognition, or emotional response.

## 4. The visual reference specification

For a major character who may need illustration or image generation, create a concise specification that reduces visual drift:

**Identity anchors**
- Character name and story/version.
- Age and apparent age, where relevant.
- Stable physical foundation and silhouette.
- Exact facial architecture and distinctive features.
- Hair texture, color, shape, and length.
- Skin tone and visible marks.
- Three to six recognition anchors.

**Design presentation**
- Neutral full-body view, front / side / back where appropriate.
- Face close-up with neutral expression.
- Expression set showing a small number of story-relevant emotions.
- Main outfit with clear materials and layers.
- Alternate outfits only when needed, each separately specified.
- Accessories, equipment, and visible markings.
- Lighting and background instructions that do not obscure the design.
- Explicit exclusions: features that must not be added, altered, or exaggerated.

**Consistency instructions**
- Preserve the same face shape, hair construction, eye color if established, body proportions, skin tone, marks, and accessories across views.
- Do not change age, ethnicity, body type, outfit, or distinguishing features between images unless the requested variation requires it.
- Mark unknown details as undecided rather than inventing them.
- For transformation or alternate forms, document which features remain constant and which change.
- Use a character-specific reference rather than relying on a name alone.

The reference specification should be visually actionable, not an ornate block of adjectives. Separate fixed design facts from optional stylistic interpretation.

## 5. Appearance continuity ledger

Maintain a per-character record with these columns or equivalent fields:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Fixed anchors | Stable body, face, hair, marks, and recognition features |
| Current outfit | Exact clothing and accessories for the current scene |
| Physical state | Cleanliness, sweat, wetness, injuries, fatigue, and other temporary states |
| Recent changes | Haircut, new scar, damage, transformation, aging, or changed equipment |
| Visibility | What is exposed, concealed, obscured, or out of view |
| Source confidence | Canon fact, project canon, inference, working plan, or unknown |
| Last verified | Scene/chapter where the current state was confirmed |

After any scene that changes the body, clothes, injury, hairstyle, disguise, or equipment, update the ledger before the next scene relies on it.

## 6. Description dosage by scene

- **First appearance:** orient the reader with a few strong anchors, then continue the scene.
- **Ordinary return:** use one reminder or a changed detail, not a full repeated description.
- **Emotional intimacy:** allow attention to narrow onto a specific detail with emotional relevance.
- **Threat or confrontation:** emphasize posture, movement, space, hands, expression, and visible readiness.
- **Combat:** emphasize actionable details, silhouette, reach, balance, equipment, injury, and spatial readability.
- **Disguise or mistaken identity:** establish what has changed, what remains recognizable, and why observers do or do not notice.
- **Transformation or major redesign:** clearly establish the new state and track which continuity locks persist.
- **Group scene:** give each important character a distinct visual anchor so readers can track who is where.

## 7. Anti-patterns to reject

- Generic descriptions that could fit dozens of characters.
- A giant inventory of features that pauses the story.
- Describing every character from head to toe at first sight.
- Contradictory details across chapters or visual references.
- Treating clothing as decoration that ignores climate, movement, class, culture, or profession.
- Using complexion as a shortcut for personality or morality.
- Confusing a temporary expression or injury with a permanent feature.
- Relying on eye color alone to distinguish a character.
- Making all attractive characters attractive in the same way.
- Overloading every character with scars, heterochromia, jewelry, tattoos, or signature accessories.
- Describing features the viewpoint cannot see or know.
- Changing established source canon without explicitly recording a divergence.
- Giving an artist or image model a name without the stable visual anchors needed to reproduce the character.

## 8. Required output for a newly created major character

Unless the project explicitly uses a lighter format, create all three:

### Deliverable A: Full Appearance Dossier
A comprehensive reference using the layers above, with stable facts, contextual variations, and unresolved details clearly marked.

### Deliverable B: Reader-Facing Description
One polished paragraph or short passage showing how the character could be introduced naturally from a specific viewpoint. It should sound like fiction, not a character sheet.

### Deliverable C: Visual Reference Specification
A concise, unambiguous design brief for consistent portraits or illustrations, including fixed anchors, outfit details, view requirements, and exclusions.

For recurring characters, update these deliverables when a meaningful design change occurs. Do not silently regenerate the whole design every time the character appears.

## 9. Final quality check

Before a major appearance is considered finished, verify:
- Can a reader imagine the character without relying on generic adjectives?
- Are face, hair, body, skin, movement, clothing, and distinguishing details specific enough?
- Do the details form one coherent design?
- Does the design fit the world and the character's life?
- Are stable facts separated from temporary states?
- Can the character be recognized from silhouette or a few anchors?
- Does the prose reveal only what the viewpoint can perceive and what the scene needs?
- Does the description feel natural rather than like a checklist?
- Are continuity locks recorded and respected?
- Could an artist or image model reproduce the intended design without guessing?

**Standard:** Design completely; describe selectively; track changes precisely.

# The Living Story Method (LSM)
**Version:** 1.0.0  
**Purpose:** A reusable, genre-flexible writing system for producing fiction that feels inhabited, emotionally credible, vivid, causally coherent, and alive across scenes, chapters, and long-running series.

This file is a local, portable copy. It is designed to travel with the project so a writing assistant can use it without depending on an external repository or remembering instructions from another chat.

## 1. The governing principle

A story feels alive when people with independent desires make consequential choices inside a world that pushes back, while the narration controls information, attention, emotion, and rhythm with intention.

Do not optimize for beautiful sentences alone. Optimize for a convincing experience: **someone wants something; something resists; choices change the situation; the change matters.**

## 2. The eight engines

1. **Human Engine:** desire, fear, need, contradiction, habit, history, agency, relationships, and self-deception.
2. **Scene Engine:** a concrete objective, resistance, tactics, escalation, a turn, and consequences.
3. **Immersion Engine:** physical staging, sensory specificity, embodied emotion, environmental pressure, and selective detail.
4. **Voice Engine:** distinct character voices, purposeful dialogue, subtext, silence, and narrative voice.
5. **World Engine:** consistent physical, social, institutional, economic, historical, informational, technological, and supernatural rules.
6. **Momentum Engine:** promises, pressure, progress, setbacks, revelations, payoffs, and deliberate variation in pace.
7. **Continuity Engine:** canon, chronology, knowledge boundaries, established consequences, unresolved questions, and source confidence.
8. **Resonance Engine:** emotional change, relationship movement, thematic tension, recurring motifs, and aftermath.

A scene or chapter need not emphasize all eight equally. It should know which engines are driving and avoid accidentally disabling the others.

## 3. Twelve laws

1. Every major character wants something, even if the desire is petty, confused, hidden, or self-contradictory.
2. Supporting characters have lives, motives, limits, and decisions beyond serving the protagonist.
3. The world pushes back. Resources, institutions, bodies, time, status, geography, rules, and other people create resistance.
4. Actions have consequences. Important actions alter danger, trust, knowledge, resources, relationships, status, or future options.
5. Emotion changes behavior. It affects attention, timing, interpretation, speech, memory, risk tolerance, and physical response.
6. Description earns its place by grounding space, revealing character, shaping mood, clarifying action, or planting meaningful information.
7. Dialogue is action. Characters persuade, conceal, test, threaten, comfort, bargain, evade, perform, or seek connection.
8. Knowledge is not universal. A character can use only what they know, reasonably infer, or deliberately gamble on.
9. The story remembers. Injuries, promises, embarrassment, losses, discoveries, and changed loyalties remain consequential until addressed.
10. Progress does not require victory. A failed attempt can reveal a rule, worsen a position, expose a motive, or force a better strategy.
11. Major developments must be earned. Foreshadowing, causality, preparation, character choice, and consequence should support important turns.
12. Prose serves the intended experience. Clarity, rhythm, density, humor, lyricism, and bluntness are tools, not universal goals.

## 4. Scene construction

Use this nine-part model as a diagnostic, not a rigid formula:

1. **Orientation:** Where are we, who is present, and what is physically happening?
2. **Desire:** What does the viewpoint character want right now?
3. **Friction:** What person, fact, limitation, environment, or internal conflict obstructs it?
4. **Tactics:** What does each participant try, and how do tactics differ?
5. **Escalation:** Why does the current approach become insufficient, costly, or dangerous?
6. **Turn:** What changes the scene's direction, understanding, leverage, or emotional temperature?
7. **Consequence:** What becomes newly possible, impossible, costly, or necessary?
8. **Residue:** What feeling, image, question, wound, suspicion, or relational shift remains?
9. **Transition:** What pulls the reader into the next scene or gives a deliberate reason to pause?

A quiet scene can have low external stakes but high relational or informational stakes. A fight is still a scene: goals, terrain, timing, decisions, costs, reversals, and consequences must remain legible.

## 5. Character vitality

For every major character, track:
- **Public surface:** what others can observe.
- **Active objective:** what they are trying to achieve now.
- **Private pressure:** fear, need, shame, obligation, longing, or risk they may conceal.
- **Contradiction:** the gap between self-image and behavior, desire and principle, or words and actions.
- **Adaptive strategy:** how they pursue goals when their first approach fails.
- **Limits:** skills, resources, knowledge, physical constraints, blind spots, and costs.
- **Relationships:** what they want from each person and what they refuse to admit.
- **Change pressure:** what could plausibly make them adapt, harden, break, trust, retreat, or double down.

Do not make every character eloquent, self-aware, rational, or emotionally articulate. People misread situations, interrupt themselves, act from habit, hide behind jokes, overreact, underreact, and understand their own motives imperfectly. These behaviors should fit the person and moment, not be random quirks.

Give supporting characters off-screen priorities and the ability to surprise the protagonist. Do not make them stupid solely to make the protagonist impressive. Competence should be demonstrated through decisions and consequences, not repeated declarations.

## 6. POV and narrative perspective engine

Before drafting, explicitly choose:
- **Narrative person:** first, second, or third person.
- **Viewpoint mode:** limited, omniscient, objective/external, or another deliberate mode.
- **Viewpoint character:** whose experience filters this passage.
- **Narrative distance:** intimate interiority, moderate access, or distant observation.
- **Information permissions:** what the narration and viewpoint character may know or reveal now.

### Deep third-person limited
Filter perception, vocabulary, judgment, attention, and emotional interpretation through the viewpoint character. The narration may be grammatically third person while carrying that character's mental habits. Do not casually reveal another character's private thoughts as fact.

### First person
Keep the narrator's knowledge, memory, bias, vocabulary, and reliability consistent. Distinguish what happened from what the narrator believes happened. Do not grant the narrator information they could not perceive or learn.

### Omniscient
Use a stable, intentional narrator with legitimate access to multiple minds. Signal shifts through narrative authority, transitions, thematic framing, or clear paragraph/scene design. Do not drift into omniscience accidentally.

### POV switches
A switch must earn its space by providing a meaningful new emotional lens, crucial information, dramatic irony, a parallel action, or a consequence the current viewpoint cannot deliver. Prefer scene or chapter boundaries. For a mid-scene switch, make the boundary unmistakable and the benefit substantial.

At every switch:
1. Establish whose experience governs the new passage promptly.
2. Change the sensory and interpretive lens, not merely the name in the paragraph.
3. Preserve knowledge asymmetry: characters must not know facts only the reader or another viewpoint has learned.
4. Do not replay the same event from another viewpoint unless the second pass materially changes interpretation, stakes, or information.
5. Track each viewpoint's location, objective, knowledge, emotional state, and unresolved action.
6. Give each viewpoint a distinct voice and attention pattern.

### POV planning ledger
For each scene, record: scene ID; viewpoint; location/time; immediate objective; what the viewpoint knows; what remains hidden; emotional starting state; narrative distance; reason this viewpoint is best; and information newly revealed.

## 7. Dialogue, subtext, and voice

Before writing a line, ask what the speaker is doing with it. A line can perform multiple actions. Let characters have different rhythms, levels of directness, humor, formality, metaphor habits, vocabulary, and tolerance for silence.

- Use tags and action beats to clarify speakers and physical behavior without choreographing every line.
- Let subtext arise from competing goals, social risk, history, power, affection, shame, and incomplete honesty.
- Allow pauses, evasions, failed jokes, corrections, interruptions, and silence when the moment warrants them.
- Do not make every exchange a witty comeback or every character a mouthpiece for the author.
- Avoid dialogue that merely repeats information all speakers already know unless repetition has a social or emotional purpose.
- Keep dialogue readable during action. Make clear who acts, where bodies are, what changes, and what each action costs.

## 8. Immersion and physical truth

Ground scenes with selective, concrete details. Use sensory information when it affects orientation, mood, threat, memory, desire, or action. Sensory detail is more than adding smells to every paragraph.

Track bodies in space: entrances, exits, distance, obstacles, sightlines, hands, posture, objects, injuries, clothing constraints, sound sources, and movement. During action, maintain cause and effect. The reader should understand what each participant attempts, what happens, and why the outcome follows.

Show emotion through a combination of thought, attention, body, behavior, language, avoidance, and consequence. Avoid relying on a stock gesture every time an emotion appears. Do not force physical symptoms when a quieter behavioral change is more credible.

## 9. Worldbuilding and systems

A world feels real when its rules affect ordinary life, not only exposition or combat. Consider:
- Physical environment and geography.
- Social norms, family, class, status, reputation, and belonging.
- Institutions, laws, organizations, incentives, and enforcement.
- Economics, labor, resources, trade, scarcity, and access.
- History, cultural memory, myths, and competing accounts.
- Information: who knows what, how they learned it, and what is concealed.
- Technology, magic, powers, supernatural phenomena, and their limits.

For every important ability or system, track prerequisites, scope, costs, limits, failure modes, counters, detection, training, consequences, and what characters reasonably understand about it. A power should not solve a problem outside its established scope without a prepared explanation. Costs must matter in the current story, not merely appear in a reference sheet.

Prefer dramatized worldbuilding: show rules through choices, routines, conflict, objects, prices, institutions, and consequences. Exposition is welcome when it answers a live question at the right moment.

## 10. Momentum and pacing

Use **Promise → Pressure → Progress → Payoff**:
- **Promise:** establish a question, goal, threat, desire, mystery, or expectation.
- **Pressure:** make the question matter and make easy solutions fail.
- **Progress:** change the situation through decisions, discoveries, losses, or new leverage.
- **Payoff:** deliver a satisfying result, reversal, revelation, or meaningful partial answer, while opening the next question where appropriate.

Vary scene modes: action, investigation, negotiation, intimacy, humor, aftermath, planning, wonder, and reflection. Fast pacing is not the same as constant fighting or short paragraphs. Slow pacing is not the same as filler. A scene earns its length through meaningful change, pressure, discovery, or emotional texture.

Use chapter endings intentionally: consequence, decision, revelation, danger, emotional turn, irony, or a question with genuine pull. Do not force a cliffhanger if a quiet resolution is the stronger beat.

## 11. Emotional resonance and theme

Track both external plot movement and internal/relational movement. After major events, ask what changed in trust, fear, desire, self-image, power balance, belonging, resentment, hope, or willingness to cooperate. Not every scene needs a large emotional speech; a small change in what someone permits, remembers, or avoids can carry weight.

Themes should emerge through choices, contradictions, costs, recurring images, and outcomes. Do not stop the story to announce its lesson. Motifs should evolve in meaning when repeated.

## 12. Continuity, canon, and source discipline

Separate these statuses explicitly:
- **Confirmed canon/project fact:** directly established by authoritative story material or approved decision.
- **Source evidence:** what an external canon/source actually depicts or states.
- **Inference:** a reasoned interpretation, labeled as such.
- **Working plan:** a proposal, not an event that has already happened.
- **Open question:** not yet decided or verified.
- **Speculation:** possible but unsupported.
- **Draft:** prose not yet approved as canon.
- **Approved/canon:** status granted under the project's own approval rules.

Never silently promote inference, a draft, an outline, or an assistant invention into canon. When sources conflict, identify the conflict and follow the project's documented authority hierarchy. Do not invent quotations, exact source events, dates, power limits, character knowledge, or details that should be verified. Read the actual relevant source and prior prose when available; summaries are navigation aids, not substitutes for required full reading.

Maintain a continuity ledger for: chronology; locations; injuries and resources; promises and debts; relationship changes; secrets and who knows them; power use and costs; unresolved plot threads; established facts; and consequences still owed. Track negative constraints too: what has not happened, what a character cannot yet do, what must not be foreshadowed prematurely, and which files or chapters are protected.

## 13. Drafting workflow

### Before the chapter
1. Read the project's governing instructions and authority hierarchy.
2. Read the actual previous chapter in full, plus any required earlier scenes and relevant source material.
3. Check the continuity ledger, character knowledge, timeline, open threads, and protected material.
4. Define the chapter's dramatic purpose, emotional movement, key decisions, scene sequence, POV assignments, and ending.
5. Identify canon-locked events versus areas where invention is permitted.

### During drafting
1. Write scenes as continuous experiences, not summaries of planned beats.
2. Give every important participant a motive and a tactic.
3. Stage physical action and dialogue clearly.
4. Track what each viewpoint can perceive, know, and infer.
5. Let new developments emerge from setup, choices, pressure, and consequences.
6. Preserve the project's established voice, format, pacing target, and approval process.

### After drafting
Run these passes in order:
1. **Structure:** does the chapter change the situation and fulfil its purpose?
2. **Character:** do choices, reactions, and voices fit the individuals?
3. **Scene vitality:** are goals, resistance, tactics, turns, and consequences present?
4. **Immersion:** can the reader orient themselves and feel the scene?
5. **Dialogue:** does each exchange do something beyond delivering information?
6. **POV:** are viewpoint, distance, switches, and knowledge boundaries controlled?
7. **Continuity:** are chronology, abilities, facts, injuries, relationships, and source claims correct?
8. **Prose:** remove vagueness, redundancy, accidental repetition, and decorative clutter; retain deliberate style.
9. **Restraint:** remove unearned drama, forced jokes, artificial cliffhangers, excessive explanation, and power inflation.
10. **Project compliance:** ensure the draft obeys repository-specific canon, source, formatting, and approval rules.

## 14. Scene quality gate

A scene is ready when the answers are clear or intentionally left open:
- Who wants what, and why now?
- What resists them?
- What does each participant try?
- What changes by the end?
- What does the reader learn, feel, suspect, or anticipate?
- Is the physical staging understandable?
- Is the POV choice useful and consistent?
- Does the scene respect what each character knows?
- Are world rules and costs obeyed?
- Does the scene create, resolve, or deliberately defer a meaningful thread?

Not every scene needs combat, a twist, a new fact, or a high-stakes reversal. It does need a reason to exist.

## 15. Chapter quality gate

- The chapter continues from the actual prior ending, not an imagined or summarized version.
- The chapter has a clear dramatic and emotional shape.
- Character decisions cause events; events do not merely happen to characters.
- Major turns are prepared and consequential.
- POV choices and switches are planned and readable.
- Every important character retains agency.
- Description, humor, action, and reflection serve the intended tone.
- Continuity, canon, chronology, and knowledge boundaries have been checked.
- The ending delivers an appropriate payoff or deliberate transition.
- The draft follows this project's own approval and preservation rules.

## 16. Genre adaptation

- **Fanfiction/reconstruction:** source fidelity and character integrity come first; label canon events, AU changes, inference, and invention separately.
- **Progression fantasy:** make advancement legible; preserve limits, costs, training, strategy, and social consequences; do not make every obstacle a power check.
- **Horror:** manage what is known, what is felt, and what remains uncertain; preserve vulnerability and consequence.
- **Science fiction/cyberpunk:** connect technology to labor, inequality, incentives, embodiment, surveillance, and daily life; keep systems coherent.
- **Romance/relationship-driven fiction:** track reciprocal desire, boundaries, misinterpretation, vulnerability, and meaningful shifts in trust.
- **Game narrative:** align scene and character logic with player agency, quest goals, gameplay readability, and the difference between authored canon and optional paths.
- **Mystery/thriller:** track clue visibility, information fairness, competing explanations, and the causal chain behind revelations.

## 17. AI operating rules

The assistant is responsible for applying the method, noticing missing information, checking the relevant project files, and proposing solutions rather than making the author invent every beat. It must not ask the author to repeat information already available in the repository or conversation.

When something is uncertain, investigate first. If it remains uncertain, label it and avoid presenting a guess as fact. Preserve protected material. Do not upload, commit, publish, approve, or canonize work unless the user has authorized that action under the project's rules. A general writing-method instruction never overrides project-specific constraints.

Do not display the checklist as a substitute for writing. Use it internally to produce fluent, engaging prose. The finished story should feel effortless to read even when the underlying planning is rigorous.


## Required companion modules: character creation and appearance
The LSM's Human, Voice, Immersion, and Continuity Engines are supported by two dedicated procedures:
- `WRITING_METHOD/ENGINES/CHARACTER_FORGE.md`: comprehensive character creation, psychology, agency, limits, relationships, voice, pressure testing, and character arcs.
- `WRITING_METHOD/ENGINES/LIVING_APPEARANCE_METHOD.md`: full appearance dossiers, natural viewpoint-filtered descriptions, visual reference specifications, and appearance continuity.

For a newly created major character, use both modules. Maintain a full Character Forge dossier and Full Appearance Dossier, write a separate Reader-Facing Description, and prepare a Visual Reference Specification when useful. Do not substitute generic adjectives for a concrete visual design. Use selective description in story prose, and track stable features separately from temporary conditions or changes.

These modules do not override project canon, source accuracy, user-approved designs, character locks, or local authority rules. Unknown details remain unknown until deliberately established.
